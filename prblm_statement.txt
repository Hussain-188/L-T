Evidence-Grounded Divergence and Convergence Engine for Competing Hypotheses
AI
Challenge Statement
Build a reasoning engine that works in two stages. First, it generates a small set of genuinely different candidate explanations for a problem, plus an explicit "unknown" option. They must differ in the cause proposed, the assumptions made, the evidence required or the effects predicted - not merely in wording. For each one, the engine lists what evidence supports it, what contradicts it, what is missing, and which single test would best separate it from the others. Second, it scores the candidates using a documented method and updates those scores as evidence arrives, without counting two pieces of evidence from the same original source as independent confirmation. The engine must end with one of four outcomes: pick the best-supported explanation, combine compatible ones, recommend a specific test, or openly decline to answer. It must report an honest confidence level and work within a fixed exploration budget with a clear rule for when to stop.

Background
When a problem is unclear, an AI usually replies with one smooth, confident explanation. Even if you ask for alternatives, it tends to return the same idea reworded. A skilled diagnostician deliberately puts forward genuinely different possible causes, states what each one assumes, looks for evidence that would prove or disprove each, and settles on an answer only when the evidence justifies it. Broadening the search should mean exploring truly different possibilities, not generating more text. Narrowing down should happen because evidence ruled things out, not because one explanation sounded more convincing. The system must also be allowed to keep "none of the above" on the table and to say "I cannot tell yet" when nothing is well supported. A strong reasoning system is one that can explain which options it considered, what evidence changed its mind, what it discarded, and why.

Deliverables
• A divergence engine producing substantive hypotheses plus unknown or null. • A multi-axis method for measuring hypothesis distinctness. • Structured hypothesis records containing assumptions and predicted effects. • Supporting, contradicting, missing and dependent-evidence handling. • A documented hypothesis-scoring and confidence-update method. • Selection, synthesis, discriminating-test or abstention output. • A fixed branch budget and stopping rule. • Calibration and selective-accuracy evaluation on held-out cases. • Failure tests for duplicate evidence, tied hypotheses and an unlisted cause.


Uncertainty-Aware Causal World Model and Intervention Simulation Engine
AI
Challenge Statement
Build an uncertainty-aware causal model of a system, working against a simulator or similar environment where the true outcomes are known. The model should support four clearly separated abilities: predicting the next state from the current one; predicting the next state given a chosen action; estimating what happens under deliberate interventions, where a variable is forced to a value regardless of what would naturally occur; and answering a "what if" question about a recorded episode by changing a past action while keeping that episode's other conditions fixed. The model may be learned, rule-based or a mix, but it must state its assumptions about causes, what can be observed, and randomness. Every prediction must carry an uncertainty range or a probability spread. Teams should compare predictions against actual outcomes, identify the conditions under which the model is unreliable, and demonstrate that a pattern in the data is not being mistaken for genuine cause and effect.

Background
Predicting what usually happens is very different from predicting what will happen if you deliberately change something. A normal prediction model spots patterns in past data - for example, that ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise together - without understanding what actually causes what. That is fine for forecasting, but useless the moment someone asks "what happens if we turn this valve down?" or "would the machine still have failed if we had replaced that part last month?" To answer those, a model must represent the real mechanisms of a system: its variables, the actions that can be taken, how one state leads to the next, what can be observed, and how much is genuinely uncertain. The aim is not a general model of the world, but one solid model that predicts, compares deliberate actions, reports honest uncertainty, and answers a "what if we had done it differently" question. Such a question is not just another simulation run - it means re-running the same episode with one action changed while keeping everything else about it the same.

Deliverables
• A world model implemented against the supplied simulator. • Documented state, action, observation and intervention definitions. • Next-state and action-conditioned predictions. • Predictions for at least two controlled interventions. • One bounded counterfactual with explicit assumptions. • Prediction uncertainty or outcome distributions. • Separate forecasting, action, intervention and counterfactual evaluation. • A calibration or probabilistic-scoring report. • Failure demonstrations for low-data conditions, missing observations and observational-interventional disagreement. • Documentation of unreliable operating regions and model limitations.


Efficient and User-Controllable Context Memory for Long-Running Agentic AI
AI
Challenge Statement
Build a context memory system for a long-running AI agent that is both efficient and transparent. The system should decide automatically what must be kept word-for-word, what can be reduced to a short summary, and what can safely be discarded, working across hundreds or thousands of messages spanning many sessions without depending on one specific model. Every such decision must be recorded rather than hidden: the memory should be saved as a chain of versioned steps, each labelled with the topic being worked on, so the user can see what is currently held, trace how the agent's understanding evolved, restart the task from any earlier point, branch off to try a different direction, and merge branches back together. Users should also be able to trim the context down or expand it again by hand, and the system should explain what it kept, what it dropped, and why. Success means a measurable reduction in tokens, cost and response time, with answer quality holding steady or improving, and with the user able to inspect and correct the agent's memory at any point instead of restarting.

Background
A language model reads everything it is given as "tokens" - roughly, pieces of words - and every token costs money and time. An agent working with the same user over weeks or months builds up a long history of conversations, documents and tool results. Most systems send all of it back to the model every time, making replies slow and expensive, until eventually the history no longer fits the model's context window and material is cut off at random. Shrinking the history is the obvious fix, but cut too much and the agent forgets preferences, drifts from the original objective and answers wrongly. A second problem compounds the first: none of this is visible. Users cannot see what the agent is remembering, cannot tell when it went off track, and can only start again. Combining efficient compression with a visible, versioned history would make long-running agents both affordable and trustworthy.

Deliverables
A working proof-of-concept demonstrating efficient, user-visible context memory across a long multi-session conversation. Deliverables should include: (1) an architecture covering memory storage, compression, retrieval and context reconstruction; (2) implementation of the versioned context chain with trace-back, restart, branch and merge; (3) an interface for inspecting, trimming and expanding context, with explanations of what was kept or dropped; (4) an evaluation comparing baseline and optimised systems on tokens, cost, latency and answer quality; (5) a demonstration of recovering from drift by returning to an earlier context version.

AI

Background

AI
Challenge Statement

Unified Multimodal Evidence Representation with Provenance, Temporal Alignment and Conflict Detection
AI
Challenge Statement
Build an engine that reads information arriving in several different forms - such as text, images, tables or sensor readings over time - and turns it into one structured, inspectable picture per case: what is involved, what happened, what was measured, in what units, at what time, and how it all relates. Every fact must be stored with a pointer back to its exact source location, along with when it applies, how confident the system is, and whether it was read directly or worked out. The engine should recognise the same real-world item across different sources even when it is named differently, place events on a shared timeline, and flag a conflict only when two facts genuinely describe the same thing at the same time in comparable terms. Background
Real systems produce information in many forms at once: written reports, photographs, tables of readings, and sensor data recorded over time. Most AI tools convert all of this into numeric "embeddings" so it can be searched by similarity. That helps find related content, but it discards information needed in order to trust an answer - which document a fact came from, which exact sentence or table cell, which item it refers to, and at what time. A maintenance report may say a machine ran normally on a particular day while the temperature sensor from that same day shows an overheating spike. A trustworthy system should show both statements and flag the clash, not quietly average them into one meaningless middle value. Deliverables
• A working engine processing text, image and structured or time-series data. • A unified state schema containing entities, events, attributes and temporal context. • A ClaimRecord schema separating extracted and inferred claims. • Claim-level evidence pointers for all supported modalities. • Cross-modal entity linking, timeline alignment and conflict detection. • Confidence reporting with accuracy and calibration evaluation. • An interface for inspecting claims and supporting evidence. • Demonstrations covering missing data, entity ambiguity and cross-modal disagreement.

AI
Challenge Statement

Non-Invasive Knee Movement Tracking for Gait Assessment
MedTech
Challenge Statement
Design and validate a non-invasive system that measures six-degree-of-freedom knee kinematics during functional movement, and present the output in a form a clinician can interpret. Teams may combine wearable inertial measurement units, depth or multi-camera vision, flexible strain sensing, or other non-invasive modalities, together with a sensor-fusion algorithm that resolves rotation and translation simultaneously. The system must operate outside a dedicated gait laboratory, tolerate soft-tissue movement, and remain usable by a single operator. Teams are expected to define a movement protocol (for example gait, sit-to-stand, stair descent and a controlled pivot), acquire data from healthy volunteers, and validate their output against an accepted reference such as an optical motion-capture system or an instrumented rig. Accuracy targets: rotational error within 5 degrees and translational error within 5 mm root-mean-square against the reference, sustained across the full movement cycle. Implantable or in-vivo sensing is explicitly out of scope; ligament-level strain measurement may be discussed only as future work.

Background
The human knee is not a simple hinge. It moves with six degrees of freedom: flexion-extension, internal-external rotation, abduction-adduction, and anterior-posterior, medial-lateral and superior-inferior translation. Clinical assessment today captures only a fraction of this. Goniometers and standard gait labs report flexion angle reliably but characterise rotation and translation poorly, and marker-based optical systems suffer from soft-tissue artefact at the thigh and shank. As a result, clinicians assessing ACL deficiency, meniscal injury, osteoarthritis progression or post-operative recovery work with an incomplete picture of joint behaviour, particularly during dynamic tasks such as stair descent, pivoting and squatting where instability actually presents. Rehabilitation protocols and implant design decisions are therefore based largely on sagittal-plane data. A low-cost, non-invasive system that resolves all six degrees of freedom during functional movement would give clinicians objective, repeatable evidence of joint mechanics, support earlier detection of instability, and allow rehabilitation progress to be measured rather than estimated.

Deliverables
The developed system to demonstrate the ability to directly measure skeletal kinematics in 6-DOF despite soft tissue deformations between the transducer and the bony surface.

Challenge Statement

Variations in slice orientation, spacing, thickness, image resolution, patient movement and acquisition quality should be appropriately considered so that they do not significantly affect the final anatomical representation. The reconstructed 3D volume should support identification and separation of relevant anatomical structures present within the selected region. The solution should also be capable of refining the reconstructed structures and generating an anatomically meaningful 3D mesh that closely represents the original patient anatomy. Particular importance should be given to alignment accuracy, structural continuity, anatomical consistency and preservation of clinically relevant features. The developed approach should further provide an interactive visualization of the reconstructed anatomical model and enable the analysis of important structural or tissue-related characteristics wherever applicable. The overall objective is to achieve a consistent, accurate and usable 3D anatomical representation that can support medical visualization, research analysis, diagnostic assessment, treatment planning and surgical planning.

Background
Medical imaging technologies such as CT, MRI, X-ray generate a large amount of anatomical information in the form of DICOM image slices. In most cases, these images are still reviewed as a sequence of 2D slices and understanding the complete 3D anatomical structure depends heavily on the experience of the clinician or researcher. When the anatomical region is complex, such as the heart, brain or breast, it becomes difficult to accurately understand the spatial relationship between different structures only from individual image slices. Another challenge is that DICOM images may have variations in slice thickness, spacing, patient orientation, image quality and acquisition parameters. Patient movement and imaging artifacts can also introduce misalignment between slices. These variations can affect the accuracy of 3D reconstruction and subsequent anatomical analysis. There is therefore a need to develop an approach that can process DICOM image data, maintain the correct anatomical and spatial relationship between image slices, reconstruct the region into an accurate 3D representation, and identify individual anatomical structures within it. The reconstructed model should also support detailed visualization and analysis of structural and functional characteristics, so that it can be useful for medical research, diagnostic assessment, treatment planning and surgical planning.

Deliverables
The expected deliverables should include a working prototype capable of processing DICOM image data and reconstructing a patient specific 3D anatomical model. The solution should demonstrate DICOM metadata handling, slice ordering and alignment, 3D volume reconstruction, anatomical structure segmentation, mesh generation and validation. Participants should provide an interactive visualization of the reconstructed model along with measurable accuracy and performance results. The final submission should also include the proposed methodology, system architecture, validation approach, test results, identified limitations and a demonstration using representative medical imaging datasets.
